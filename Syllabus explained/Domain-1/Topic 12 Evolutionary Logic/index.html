TOPIC 12

EVOLUTIONARY LOGIC

Uracil vs. Thymine: From One Methyl Group to Mutation Calling

1. WHY THIS MATTERS

DNA and RNA differ by only a few chemical choices, but those choices determine what kinds of damage can occur and how computational systems interpret sequence.

One of the most important examples is:

\( \boxed{\text{Uracil} \leftrightarrow \text{Thymine}} \)

The structural difference is deceptively small:

\( \boxed{\text{Thymine} = \text{Uracil} + \mathrm{CH_3}} \)

That methyl group does not simply make thymine “more stable.”

Its deeper importance is that it gives DNA a way to distinguish:

a legitimate pyrimidine from a chemically damaged base.

That distinction eventually affects:

\( \text{DNA chemistry} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{repair} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{replication} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{mutation} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{sequencing} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{variant calling} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{mutation signatures} \)

So this is not merely a molecular-biology detail.

It is part of the physical origin of information that bioinformatics later treats as a letter in a sequence.

2. START AT THE ATOMIC LEVEL

Before comparing U and T, we need to understand what a pyrimidine is.

Pyrimidines are single-ring nitrogen-containing heterocyclic bases.

The major biological pyrimidines are:

\( \boxed{C,\ U,\ T} \)

where:

* C = cytosine * U = uracil * T = thymine

Their structures are closely related.

Simplified structural relationship

\( \text{Cytosine} \\ \xrightarrow{\text{deamination}} \\ \text{Uracil} \)

and:

\( \text{Uracil} \\ \xrightarrow{\text{C5 methylation}} \\ \text{Thymine} \)

So these bases occupy closely related chemical states.

3. WHAT EXACTLY IS DIFFERENT BETWEEN U AND T?

The difference occurs at carbon 5 of the pyrimidine ring.

Uracil contains:

\mathrm{-H}

at C5.

Thymine contains:

\mathrm{-CH_3}

at C5.

Therefore:

\( \boxed{T = U + CH_3} \)

More explicitly:

\text{Uracil: } C_4H_4N_2O_2

\text{Thymine: } C_5H_6N_2O_2

The additional group is:

\( \boxed{-CH_3} \)

This seems tiny.

But biologically, the important question is not:

“Does the methyl group make T chemically better?”

The more important question is:

What happens when cytosine undergoes spontaneous chemical damage?

That is where the whole system becomes interesting.

4. THE CHEMICAL VULNERABILITY OF CYTOSINE

Cytosine contains an exocyclic amino group:

\( \boxed{-NH_2} \)

This group can undergo hydrolytic deamination.

At a simplified chemical level:

\( \boxed{ \\ C + H_2O \\ \rightarrow \\ U + NH_3 \\ } \)

The exact protonation state depends on the environment, but conceptually:

cytosine loses its amino functionality and becomes uracil.

5. WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGES DURING DEAMINATION?

This is important.

We are not simply “swapping one letter.”

An atom-level chemical group changes.

Before

Cytosine contains:

-C-NH_2

After deamination

That functionality becomes carbonyl-containing:

-C=O

So:

\( \boxed{-NH_2 \rightarrow =O} \)

This changes the hydrogen-bonding properties of the base.

That is why the biological identity of the base changes.

6. CYTOSINE DEAMINATION INSIDE DNA

Imagine a normal DNA base pair:

C:G

The cytosine is chemically altered:

\( C:G \\ \rightarrow \\ U:G \)

Now we have a mismatch.

Why?

Because uracil behaves like thymine in base pairing.

Uracil preferentially pairs with adenine:

U:A

not guanine:

U:G

Therefore the original pairing information has been corrupted.

7. THE HYDROGEN-BONDING LEVEL

This is where the chemistry connects to sequence information.

Normal:

C:G

has characteristic hydrogen-bonding geometry.

After deamination:

U:G

the base is no longer chemically equivalent to cytosine.

The damaged base now has a pairing preference similar to thymine.

So the DNA repair system faces:

\( \boxed{\text{What should this U be?}} \)

Was it:

* a legitimate U? * or damaged C?

In DNA, this question can be answered because normal DNA uses T, not U.

8. THE COUNTERFACTUAL EXPERIMENT

This is the most important reasoning step.

Imagine DNA normally used U:

A,U,G,C

instead of:

A,T,G,C

Now cytosine deaminates:

\( C \\ \rightarrow \\ U \)

But U is already a legitimate DNA base.

So the damaged product is chemically indistinguishable, at the level of base identity, from a normal nucleotide.

The repair machinery cannot simply use:

\( \boxed{\text{U = damage}} \)

because:

\( \boxed{\text{U could be normal}} \)

That creates a serious information-preservation problem.

9. THYMINE CREATES AN ERROR SIGNAL

DNA instead uses:

\( \boxed{T} \)

as its normal pyrimidine partner for adenine.

Therefore:

U \text{ in DNA}

is suspicious.

This creates an elegant biochemical strategy:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{C deamination} \\ \rightarrow \\ U \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{abnormal U in DNA} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{repair} \\ } \)

The methyl group on thymine therefore participates in a damage-discrimination strategy.

It creates a distinction between:

\( \boxed{\text{normal T}} \)

and:

\( \boxed{\text{damage-derived U}} \)

10. THE PHYSICAL STATE OF THE DNA

Let’s follow what happens physically.

Initially:

DNA duplex 5' ─── C ─── 3' │ │ hydrogen bonding │ 3' ─── G ─── 5'

After deamination:

5' ─── U ─── 3' │ │ mismatched geometry │ 3' ─── G ─── 5'

The DNA is still a duplex.

It has not necessarily broken.

Instead, the chemical identity of one base has changed.

This distinction is crucial:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{DNA damage does not necessarily mean DNA strand break} \\ } \)

A single nucleotide can be chemically damaged while the backbone remains intact.

11. WHY DOES THIS MATTER FOR DNA REPAIR?

Repair enzymes can scan DNA for chemically abnormal bases.

Uracil-DNA glycosylase (UNG) is one important example.

Conceptually:

DNA ↓ scan for abnormal U ↓ recognize U ↓ remove base ↓ abasic site ↓ backbone processing ↓ insert correct nucleotide ↓ ligate

The key conceptual step is:

\( \boxed{\text{Base recognition}} \)

The enzyme does not need to infer history perfectly.

It can use the chemical identity of the base plus its DNA context.

12. BASE EXCISION REPAIR AT THE CHEMICAL LEVEL

Suppose we have:

U:G

A uracil-DNA glycosylase can hydrolyze the N-glycosidic bond connecting the base to the deoxyribose.

Conceptually:

\( DNA-U \\ \xrightarrow{\text{glycosylase}} \\ DNA\text{-abasic site} \)

The base is removed, but the sugar-phosphate backbone initially remains.

So:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{base removed} \\ \neq \\ \text{strand removed} \\ } \)

Now the DNA contains an AP site (apurinic/apyrimidinic site).

13. THE BACKBONE THEN BECOMES THE PROBLEM

An AP site is chemically unstable and is processed by downstream repair machinery.

Conceptually:

\( \text{AP site} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{phosphodiester backbone cleavage} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{gap} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{DNA synthesis} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{ligation} \)

The exact enzymes and pathway details vary between organisms.

But the information-recovery principle is:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{remove chemically wrong base} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{restore correct base} \\ } \)

14. WHAT IF REPAIR DOES NOT HAPPEN?

Now we reach the mutation mechanism.

Start:

C:G

Deamination:

\( C:G \\ \rightarrow \\ U:G \)

Suppose replication occurs before repair.

The polymerase sees U.

Because U base-pairs like T, it tends to template:

A

Therefore:

\( U:G \\ \rightarrow \\ U:A \)

After another round of replication, that can become:

T:A

The original:

C:G

has therefore become:

T:A

So:

\( \boxed{ \\ C:G \\ \rightarrow \\ U:G \\ \rightarrow \\ U:A \\ \rightarrow \\ T:A \\ } \)

This is the molecular origin of a C→T transition on one strand.

15. WHY IS IT CALLED A TRANSITION?

There are two classes of nucleotide substitutions.

Transitions

Purine → purine:

A \leftrightarrow G

or pyrimidine → pyrimidine:

C \leftrightarrow T

Transversions

Purine ↔ pyrimidine:

A/G \leftrightarrow C/T

Therefore:

\( \boxed{C\rightarrow T = \text{transition}} \)

16. THE CRITICAL DISTINCTION: DAMAGE VS MUTATION

This distinction is fundamental.

Chemical damage

\( C \rightarrow U \)

This is a lesion.

The original genomic information has not necessarily been permanently changed yet.

If repaired:

\( U:G \rightarrow C:G \)

No mutation remains.

But if replication converts the lesion into a stable base-pair change:

\( C:G \rightarrow T:A \)

then we have a:

\( \boxed{\text{mutation}} \)

So:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{damage} \neq \text{mutation} \\ } \)

Mutation can be the heritable consequence of unrepaired damage.

17. NOW ADD THE METHYL GROUP

Here is the deeper evolutionary logic.

If DNA used U:

\( C \rightarrow U \)

would produce:

U:G

but U would be a legitimate DNA base.

The repair system would have much less information about whether U was:

\text{normal}

or:

\text{damage-derived}

Using T instead means:

\( C \xrightarrow{\text{deamination}} U \)

creates an abnormal base identity.

Thus:

\( \boxed{ \\ T \text{ provides a chemical distinction between normal DNA and one important class of damage} \\ } \)

It does not prevent cytosine deamination.

It makes one consequence of that deamination easier to recognize.

18. AN IMPORTANT SECOND MECHANISM: 5-METHYLCYTOSINE

There is an especially important complication.

DNA can contain:

5\text{-methylcytosine}

which is cytosine with a methyl group at C5.

If it undergoes deamination:

\( 5mC \\ \rightarrow \\ T \)

This is very different.

Instead of:

\( C\rightarrow U \)

we have:

\( \boxed{5mC\rightarrow T} \)

The resulting base T is a normal DNA base.

Therefore:

\( 5mC:G \\ \rightarrow \\ T:G \)

creates a mismatch that is more difficult to distinguish from an ordinary T.

This is one reason methylated cytosines, particularly at CpG sites in many vertebrate genomes, are important sources of C→T mutation.

Notice the conceptual symmetry:

Unmethylated cytosine

\( C \\ \xrightarrow{\text{deamination}} \\ U \)

U is abnormal in DNA.

Methylated cytosine

\( 5mC \\ \xrightarrow{\text{deamination}} \\ T \)

T is normal in DNA.

Therefore:

\( \boxed{ \\ 5mC\text{ deamination can evade the simple “U is abnormal” strategy} \\ } \)

This is a major bridge between epigenetics, DNA damage, and mutation spectra.

19. NOW MOVE TO THE COMPUTATIONAL LEVEL

At the molecular level, we have:

\( C \\ \rightarrow \\ U \\ \rightarrow \\ T \)

But a sequencer does not normally report:

“This cytosine underwent hydrolytic deamination.”

It observes molecular products and converts them into signals that become sequence data.

So the computational pipeline begins much later.

A simplified chain is:

DNA molecule ↓ chemical damage / mutation ↓ DNA extraction ↓ library preparation ↓ sequencing ↓ instrument signal ↓ base calling ↓ FASTQ ↓ alignment ↓ BAM/CRAM ↓ variant calling ↓ VCF ↓ mutation spectrum ↓ biological interpretation

The computer is therefore seeing the consequence of molecular chemistry.

20. SEQUENCING DOES NOT SEE “C”

This is an important conceptual correction.

A FASTQ file contains:

ACGTACCGT...

But the sequencer did not directly see a letter C.

It measured some physical signal.

Depending on the technology, that might involve:

* fluorescence * electrical current * ionic changes * polymerase kinetics * other molecular signals

The computational system transforms:

\( \boxed{\text{physical signal}} \\ \rightarrow \\ \boxed{\text{base call}} \)

So even the letter “C” is already a computational abstraction.

21. THREE LEVELS OF THE SAME EVENT

This is one of the most useful mental models.

Level 1 — Molecular

\( C:G \\ \rightarrow \\ U:G \)

chemical damage.

Level 2 — Genetic

After replication:

\( C:G \\ \rightarrow \\ T:A \)

stable sequence change.

Level 3 — Computational

The reference genome contains:

...C...

while the sample contains evidence for:

...T...

A variant caller may therefore produce:

REF=C ALT=T

The VCF does not know that the molecular mechanism was cytosine deamination.

It is reporting an observed/inferred sequence difference.

This distinction is absolutely fundamental.

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{VCF representation} \neq \text{molecular mechanism} \\ } \)

22. WHAT DOES A VARIANT CALLER ACTUALLY DO?

Suppose the reference has:

Reference: A C G T A

At one genomic position, sequencing reads contain:

Read 1: A C G T A Read 2: A C G T A Read 3: A T G T A Read 4: A T G T A Read 5: A T G T A

The caller observes evidence for two alleles:

C

and:

T

It then evaluates whether the data are better explained by:

\( \boxed{\text{C/C}} \)

versus:

\( \boxed{\text{C/T}} \)

versus:

\( \boxed{\text{T/T}} \)

This becomes a statistical inference problem.

23. THE CORE STATISTICAL IDEA

Imagine:

n=100

reads cover a position.

Suppose:

80 = C

20 = T

A naive allele fraction is:

\( AF = \frac{20}{100}=0.20 \)

So:

\( \boxed{AF=20\%} \)

But the caller cannot simply say:

“20% T means mutation.”

It must consider sequencing errors.

Suppose the per-base error probability is:

\epsilon = 0.001

Then the probability of observing a T when the true base is C is approximately:

\( P(T|C)=0.001 \)

A real variant should generate substantially more T observations than expected from random sequencing error.

24. BAYESIAN VIEW OF VARIANT CALLING

Conceptually, the caller wants:

P(\text{variant}\mid\text{reads})

Using Bayes’ theorem:

\( \boxed{ \\ P(V|D) \\ = \\ \frac{P(D|V)P(V)} \\ {P(D)} \\ } \)

where:

* V = variant hypothesis * D = sequencing data

The key term is:

P(D|V)

How likely are the observed bases if a true variant exists?

Compare this with:

P(D|\neg V)

How likely are the same observations if no variant exists?

Therefore:

\( \boxed{ \\ \frac{P(D|V)}{P(D|\neg V)} \\ } \)

is essentially asking:

How much more strongly do these reads support a variant than sequencing noise?

25. WHERE DOES MOLECULAR BIOLOGY ENTER?

Now imagine we observe:

\( C\rightarrow T \)

computationally.

That alone tells us:

The sample differs from the reference at this position.

It does not prove:

Cytosine deamination caused this mutation.

To move from:

\text{variant}

to:

\text{mechanism}

we need additional biological evidence.

This is where mutation-spectrum analysis becomes powerful.

26. MUTATION SPECTRA

Instead of studying one mutation, count many substitutions.

For example:

\( C\rightarrow T \)
\( C\rightarrow A \)
\( C\rightarrow G \)
\( T\rightarrow C \)

etc.

A simple mutation spectrum might be represented as:

N_{C>T},\ N_{C>A},\ N_{C>G},\ldots

If:

N_{C>T}

is strongly enriched relative to other substitutions, that becomes biologically informative.

But context matters.

For example:

5'-CpG-3'

can behave differently from cytosine outside CpG contexts.

Thus the computational representation can become:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{substitution type + sequence context} \\ } \)

rather than simply:

C>T

27. FROM ONE MUTATION TO A MUTATIONAL PROCESS

Now we can build the complete reasoning chain:

Chemical environment ↓ DNA damage / replication error ↓ Repair or failure of repair ↓ Mutation ↓ Sequence difference ↓ Sequencing ↓ Base calls ↓ Variant calling ↓ Mutation spectrum ↓ Context-dependent pattern ↓ Inference about mutational processes

This is one of the central ideas in computational genomics:

A statistical pattern in sequence data can contain traces of the chemistry that generated the mutations.

28. WHY SEQUENCE CONTEXT MATTERS

Suppose we simply record:

\( C\rightarrow T \)

We have discarded information.

Instead, record:

5'-A[C>T]G-3'

Now the mutation has a local context.

For trinucleotide-based mutation classification, one often represents substitutions using the six pyrimidine-centered classes:

C>A

C>G

C>T

T>A

T>C

T>G

with reverse-complement normalization allowing all substitutions to be represented consistently.

The computational system can therefore transform:

\( \boxed{\text{millions of individual variants}} \)

into:

\( \boxed{\text{a structured mutation profile}} \)

29. WHY REVERSE-COMPLEMENT NORMALIZATION MATTERS

DNA is double-stranded.

Suppose one strand contains:

\( C\rightarrow T \)

The complementary strand contains:

\( G\rightarrow A \)

These are the same physical mutation viewed from opposite strands.

Therefore:

C>T

and:

G>A

can represent the same underlying molecular event.

To avoid double-counting, computational analyses often choose a canonical pyrimidine representation.

Thus:

G>A

is represented as its complementary:

C>T

This is a beautiful example of molecular structure directly influencing computational data representation.

30. THE HIDDEN ASSUMPTION IN A VCF

When we see:

REF=C ALT=T

there are multiple assumptions underneath it.

The computational pipeline has assumed that:

1. reads were generated correctly, 2. base calls are sufficiently accurate, 3. sequencing errors are modeled, 4. reads were aligned correctly, 5. the reference coordinate is correct, 6. the evidence is not caused by mapping artifacts, 7. the allele is sufficiently supported, 8. the variant model is appropriate.

Therefore:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{A variant call is an inference, not a direct observation of a mutation molecule.} \\ } \)

31. THE EVEN DEEPER HIDDEN ASSUMPTION

There is another layer.

Suppose our pipeline reports:

C>T

The computational system may treat C and T as abstract symbols.

But physically:

C

means a particular molecular structure.

And:

T

means another molecular structure.

So computational genomics performs a sequence of abstractions:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{atoms} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{molecules} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{DNA structure} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{physical signal} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{base call} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{sequence symbol} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{variant} \\ } \)

Every arrow can introduce uncertainty.

32. WHERE CAN THINGS GO WRONG?

This is where the concept becomes directly useful for bioinformatics.

Failure 1 — Chemical damage

Suppose DNA contains damaged bases.

Then:

\text{DNA molecule} \neq \text{perfect representation of original genome}

Depending on the experimental workflow, damage can contribute to misleading sequence observations.

Failure 2 — DNA degradation

If DNA is fragmented or chemically damaged during preparation, the library may become biased.

That affects:

\text{coverage}

and therefore downstream confidence.

Failure 3 — Base-calling error

The physical signal may be incorrectly converted:

\( C \rightarrow T \)

computationally.

Now the pipeline may see a false substitution.

Failure 4 — Alignment error

A read containing:

...C...

may align incorrectly.

Then the apparent:

C>T

could actually be:

\text{mapping artifact}

Failure 5 — PCR-related artifacts

Amplification can increase the representation of molecules carrying particular errors.

Then:

\text{one original error}

can appear as:

\text{many supporting reads}

This is why:

\( \boxed{\text{read count} \neq \text{independent molecular evidence}} \)

33. INDEPENDENT MOLECULES VS READS

This is a deeper computational concept.

Suppose PCR creates 20 copies from one original molecule.

We might observe:

20 reads → T

It looks extremely convincing.

But those 20 reads may represent:

\( \boxed{1\text{ original molecule}} \)

rather than:

20\text{ independent observations}

This is why sequencing pipelines care about:

* molecular duplicates * unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) * library complexity * strand evidence * read position * base quality

The computational system is trying to reconstruct:

\( \boxed{\text{How much independent physical evidence supports this sequence state?}} \)

34. CHEMISTRY → STATISTICS

This is the bridge we should remember.

A molecular event changes the probability distribution of observed data.

Suppose:

H_0 = \text{true base is C}

and:

H_1 = \text{true base is T}

The sequencer gives observations:

D = \{C,C,T,C,T,\ldots\}

The computational system calculates how compatible D is with each hypothesis.

Thus:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{chemistry determines possible molecular states} \\ } \)

while:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{statistics determines how strongly observed data support those states} \\ } \)

35. CHEMISTRY → INFORMATION THEORY

There is an even deeper interpretation.

DNA is an information-storage system.

Consider:

C

and:

T

as two possible symbols.

The biological system needs to preserve information about which symbol was originally present.

Cytosine deamination creates:

\( C\rightarrow U \)

which introduces uncertainty about the original state.

Repair reduces that uncertainty.

We can think of repair as information restoration:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{chemical damage} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{uncertainty} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{repair} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{information recovery} \\ } \)

If repair fails:

\( \text{uncertainty} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{replication} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{fixed mutation} \)

The mutation is effectively a permanent change in the stored information.

36. A PHYSICAL → COMPUTATIONAL INFORMATION CHAIN

Now combine everything:

Physical level

C:G

Chemical event

\( C + H_2O \rightarrow U + NH_3 \)

Damaged duplex

U:G

Repair

\( U:G \rightarrow C:G \)

OR failed repair

\( U:G \\ \rightarrow \\ U:A \\ \rightarrow \\ T:A \)

Biological consequence

\( C:G\rightarrow T:A \)

Sequencing

\( \text{DNA molecule} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{instrument signal} \)

Base calling

\( \text{signal}\rightarrow T \)

Alignment

\( T\rightarrow \text{genomic coordinate} \)

Variant calling

REF=C,\quad ALT=T

Population/mutation analysis

C>T

Biological interpretation

\text{possible mutational process}

This is the complete bridge.

37. WHY BIOINFORMATICIANS NEED THIS CHEMISTRY

If we only understand the computational layer, we see:

C → T

as a categorical event.

If we understand the molecular layer, we ask:

Why is C→T common in this dataset?

Then we investigate:

* sequence context * methylation * DNA repair * replication * sample preparation * sequencing technology * damage patterns * biological exposure * mutational processes

The computational pattern becomes a question about molecular causality.

38. WHAT HAPPENS IF WE DON’T UNDERSTAND THIS?

We can make a subtle but important mistake:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{observed substitution} \\ = \\ \text{biological mutation} \\ } \)

That equation is false.

The real relationship is closer to:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{observed sequence difference} \\ = \\ f( \\ \text{true genome}, \\ \text{damage}, \\ \text{library preparation}, \\ \text{sequencing}, \\ \text{alignment}, \\ \text{calling} \\ ) \\ } \)

The computational observation is therefore the output of an entire measurement system.

39. THE BIOINFORMATICS PIPELINE AS A CHAIN OF FILTERS

Think of the pipeline as progressively transforming reality.

REAL DNA │ ▼ Chemical state │ ▼ Extracted DNA │ ▼ Library molecules │ ▼ Sequenced molecules │ ▼ Instrument signals │ ▼ Base calls │ ▼ Aligned reads │ ▼ Variant evidence │ ▼ VCF │ ▼ Mutation spectrum │ ▼ Biological interpretation

Every stage is a filter.

If an upstream stage is wrong:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{downstream computation can be perfectly executed and still produce the wrong biological conclusion} \\ } \)

This is one of the most important principles in bioinformatics.

40. THE “C→T” EXAMPLE AT FOUR LEVELS

Level What is happening? Atomic Chemical functional group changes Molecular Cytosine becomes uracil Biological Repair or replication determines whether damage becomes mutation Computational Sequencing and variant calling may represent the final state as C>T

So the same event has different meanings depending on the layer.

41. THE MOST IMPORTANT EQUATIONS

Cytosine deamination

\( \boxed{C + H_2O \rightarrow U + NH_3} \)

DNA damage

\( \boxed{C:G\rightarrow U:G} \)

Repair

\( \boxed{U:G\rightarrow C:G} \)

Mutation fixation

\( \boxed{ \\ C:G \\ \rightarrow \\ U:G \\ \rightarrow \\ U:A \\ \rightarrow \\ T:A \\ } \)

Allele frequency

\( \boxed{ \\ AF=\frac{\text{ALT reads}}{\text{total informative reads}} \\ } \)

Bayesian variant inference

\( \boxed{ \\ P(V|D)= \\ \frac{P(D|V)P(V)} \\ {P(D)} \\ } \)

Likelihood comparison

\( \boxed{ \\ \frac{P(D|V)} \\ {P(D|\neg V)} \\ } \)

Mutation spectrum

\( \boxed{ \\ N_{C>T},N_{C>A},N_{C>G},N_{T>C},\ldots \\ } \)

These equations connect chemistry → probability → genomic inference.

42. THE DEEPEST MENTAL MODEL

Do not memorize:

“DNA has T and RNA has U.”

Instead, think:

DNA INFORMATION │ ▼ Cytosine is chemically damage-prone │ ▼ C ──deamination──> U │ ▼ U is abnormal in DNA │ ▼ Repair machinery │ ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐ │ │ repair no repair │ │ ▼ ▼ C:G replication │ ▼ T:A │ ▼ stable C→T mutation │ ▼ sequencing │ ▼ base calling │ ▼ alignment │ ▼ variant calling │ ▼ REF=C ALT=T │ ▼ mutation spectrum │ ▼ biological interpretation

43. THE KEY TAKEAWAY

The profound idea is not simply:

\( \boxed{T=U+CH_3} \)

It is:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{a tiny chemical difference can create an information-preservation strategy} \\ } \)

The methyl group on thymine helps create a distinction between:

\( \boxed{\text{normal DNA base}} \)

and:

\( \boxed{\text{one important chemically damaged base}} \)

That molecular distinction influences repair.

Repair influences mutation probability.

Mutation probability influences genomic variation.

Genomic variation produces sequencing observations.

Sequencing observations become computational evidence.

And computational evidence becomes biological interpretation.

So the complete bioinformatics mental model is:

\( \boxed{ \\ \text{Structure} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{Chemistry} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{Damage} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{Repair} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{Replication} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{Mutation} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{Measurement} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{Statistics} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{Variant} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{Biological inference} \\ } \)

That is why understanding the methyl group on thymine is not a small detail. It is an example of how molecular chemistry ultimately becomes computational data.
