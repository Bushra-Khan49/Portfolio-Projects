TOPIC 12

EVOLUTIONARY LOGIC

From Molecular Damage to Variant Calling

Why Are We Starting This?

In Topic 11, we saw how DNA uses Thymine to flag chemical damage (like cytosine deamination) so repair enzymes can fix it.

But what happens when that damage escapes repair and undergoes replication? A transient chemical lesion becomes a permanent genomic mutation.

We are starting this topic because the sequencer does not see "damage"—it only sees abstract letters. To truly understand bioinformatics, we must understand how a molecular accident physically transforms into a statistical variant call.

Where Does This Fit?

This topic is the bridge between physical reality and computational data. It fits exactly at the boundary where molecular biology ends and data science begins.

1. Chemistry (Spontaneous Deamination) ↓ 2. Biology (Failed Repair & Replication) ↓ 3. Technology (Sequencing the Mutation) ↓ 4. Computation (Variant Calling & VCF)

The Physical State of the DNA

Let’s follow what happens physically. Initially, we have a normal DNA duplex (C:G). After deamination, we have U:G.

The DNA is still a duplex. It has not necessarily broken. Instead, the chemical identity of one base has changed. This distinction is crucial:

\( \boxed{\text{DNA damage does not necessarily mean DNA strand break}} \)

A single nucleotide can be chemically damaged while the backbone remains intact.

Repair enzymes scan DNA for chemically abnormal bases. A uracil-DNA glycosylase can hydrolyze the bond connecting the base to the deoxyribose, leaving the sugar-phosphate backbone initially intact:

\( \boxed{\text{base removed} \neq \text{strand removed}} \)

Damage vs. Mutation

This distinction is fundamental. Chemical damage (\( C \rightarrow U \)) is a lesion. The original genomic information has not necessarily been permanently changed yet.

If repaired (\( U:G \rightarrow C:G \)), no mutation remains.

But suppose replication occurs before repair. The polymerase sees U. Because U base-pairs like T, it tends to template A. After another round of replication, that can become T:A.

\( \boxed{ C:G \rightarrow U:G \rightarrow U:A \rightarrow T:A } \)

then we have a mutation.

\( \boxed{ \text{damage} \neq \text{mutation} } \)

Mutation can be the heritable consequence of unrepaired damage.

An Important Second Mechanism: 5-Methylcytosine

There is an especially important complication. DNA can contain 5-methylcytosine, which is cytosine with a methyl group at C5.

If it undergoes deamination:

\( \boxed{5mC \rightarrow T} \)

This is very different. Instead of \( C \rightarrow U \), we have \( 5mC \rightarrow T \). The resulting base T is a normal DNA base.

Therefore, \( 5mC:G \rightarrow T:G \) creates a mismatch that is more difficult to distinguish from an ordinary T. This is one reason methylated cytosines, particularly at CpG sites in many vertebrate genomes, are important sources of C→T mutation.

Notice the conceptual symmetry:

  • Unmethylated cytosine: C undergoes deamination to U. U is abnormal in DNA.
  • Methylated cytosine: 5mC undergoes deamination to T. T is normal in DNA.
\( \boxed{ 5mC\text{ deamination can evade the simple “U is abnormal” strategy} } \)

This is a major bridge between epigenetics, DNA damage, and mutation spectra.

Sequencing Does Not See "C"

Now move to the computational level. A FASTQ file contains ACGTACCGT...

But the sequencer did not directly see a letter C. It measured some physical signal. Depending on the technology, that might involve:

  • fluorescence
  • electrical current
  • ionic changes
  • polymerase kinetics
  • other molecular signals

The computational system transforms:

\( \boxed{\text{physical signal}} \rightarrow \boxed{\text{base call}} \)

So even the letter "C" is already a computational abstraction.

Three Levels of the Same Event

This is one of the most useful mental models.

Level 1 — Molecular
\( C:G \rightarrow U:G \)
chemical damage.

Level 2 — Genetic
After replication: \( C:G \rightarrow T:A \)
stable sequence change.

Level 3 — Computational
The reference genome contains ...C... while the sample contains evidence for ...T....

A variant caller may therefore produce: REF=C, ALT=T.

The VCF does not know that the molecular mechanism was cytosine deamination. It is reporting an observed/inferred sequence difference.

\( \boxed{ \text{VCF representation} \neq \text{molecular mechanism} } \)

What Does a Variant Caller Actually Do?

Suppose the reference has A C G T A. At one genomic position, sequencing reads contain:

Read 1: A C G T A Read 2: A C G T A Read 3: A T G T A Read 4: A T G T A Read 5: A T G T A

Imagine \( n=100 \) reads cover a position. Suppose 80 = C, and 20 = T. A naive allele fraction is:

\( \boxed{AF=20\%} \)

But the caller cannot simply say “20% T means mutation.” It must consider sequencing errors. Suppose the per-base error probability is \( \epsilon = 0.001 \). Then the probability of observing a T when the true base is C is approximately 0.001.

A real variant should generate substantially more T observations than expected from random sequencing error.

Bayesian View of Variant Calling

Conceptually, the caller wants \( P(\text{variant}\mid\text{reads}) \). Using Bayes’ theorem:

\( \boxed{ P(V|D) = \frac{P(D|V)P(V)}{P(D)} } \)

where \( V \) = variant hypothesis, and \( D \) = sequencing data.

The key term is \( P(D|V) \) — How likely are the observed bases if a true variant exists? Compare this with \( P(D|\neg V) \) — How likely are the same observations if no variant exists?

Therefore:

\( \boxed{ \frac{P(D|V)}{P(D|\neg V)} } \)

is essentially asking: How much more strongly do these reads support a variant than sequencing noise?

Mutation Spectra

Instead of studying one mutation, we count many substitutions. For example: \( C\rightarrow T, C\rightarrow A, C\rightarrow G \), etc.

If \( C\rightarrow T \) is strongly enriched relative to other substitutions, that becomes biologically informative.

But context matters. Suppose we simply record \( C\rightarrow T \). We have discarded information. Instead, record:

5'-A[C>T]G-3'

Now the mutation has a local context. The computational system can therefore transform millions of individual variants into a structured mutation profile.

Reverse-Complement Normalization

DNA is double-stranded. Suppose one strand contains \( C\rightarrow T \). The complementary strand contains \( G\rightarrow A \).

These are the same physical mutation viewed from opposite strands. To avoid double-counting, computational analyses often choose a canonical pyrimidine representation.

Thus:

\( G>A \) is represented as its complementary \( C>T \)

This is a beautiful example of molecular structure directly influencing computational data representation.

The Hidden Assumptions in a VCF

When we see REF=C, ALT=T, there are multiple assumptions underneath it. The computational pipeline has assumed that:

  1. reads were generated correctly,
  2. base calls are sufficiently accurate,
  3. sequencing errors are modeled,
  4. reads were aligned correctly,
  5. the reference coordinate is correct,
  6. the evidence is not caused by mapping artifacts,
  7. the allele is sufficiently supported.
\( \boxed{ \text{A variant call is an inference, not a direct observation of a mutation molecule.} } \)

Independent Molecules vs Reads: Suppose PCR creates 20 copies from one original molecule. We might observe 20 reads → T. It looks extremely convincing. But those 20 reads may represent 1 original molecule rather than 20 independent observations. The computational system is trying to reconstruct: How much independent physical evidence supports this sequence state?

The Bioinformatics Pipeline as a Chain of Filters

Think of the pipeline as progressively transforming reality. Every stage is a filter. If an upstream stage is wrong:

\( \boxed{\text{downstream computation can be perfectly executed and still produce the wrong biological conclusion}} \)
REAL DNA │ ▼ Chemical state (Deamination C→U) │ ▼ Extracted DNA │ ▼ Library molecules (Unrepaired damage replicated) │ ▼ Sequenced molecules │ ▼ Instrument signals │ ▼ Base calls (T) │ ▼ Aligned reads │ ▼ Variant evidence (REF=C, ALT=T) │ ▼ VCF │ ▼ Mutation spectrum │ ▼ Biological interpretation

That is why understanding the methyl group on thymine is not a small detail. It is an example of how molecular chemistry ultimately becomes computational data.
