TOPIC 8

Bioinformatics Representation & Inference

Algorithms don't operate on molecules. They operate on models.

Scroll to Explore

Why This Matters

Think back to what we've established about DNA replication and molecular geometry. The molecules we track have complex physical properties.

Bioinformatics is not simply the manipulation of biological strings. It is the computational representation and analysis of physical biological systems.

A computational error does not necessarily cause a program to crash. A script can be syntactically correct while producing biologically incorrect results.

The Big Picture

We solve the "silent error" problem by understanding the chain of representations.

MOLECULE ↓ BIOLOGICAL PROCESS ↓ EXPERIMENT ↓ SEQUENCE ↓ FILE FORMAT ↓ COMPUTATIONAL OBJECT ↓ ALGORITHM ↓ STATISTICAL RESULT ↓ BIOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION

Step 1 — The Chemical Difference (DNA vs RNA)

The fundamental representation issue starts with chemistry.

It is tempting to think of DNA and RNA simply as strings of A, T/U, G, and C. But biologically, they are not just strings. They represent molecules with different chemical groups, sugar structures, and thermodynamic properties.

The most important structural difference is the sugar at the 2′ carbon.

DNA has: 2' - H RNA has: 2' - OH

That one additional oxygen-containing hydroxyl group changes hydrogen bonding, steric interactions, hydration, backbone geometry, and RNA folding.

Step 2 — The Computational Consequence

Why does one OH group matter computationally?

If your task is simply counting bases, the distinction might not matter. But if you ask: "What structure will this RNA fold into?" or "What is the free energy?", chemistry matters directly.

\[ \Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S \]

The algorithm isn't asking "Are these two letters complementary?" It is asking: "Given the chemical properties, how energetically favorable is this molecular configuration?" They come from thermodynamic models derived from experimental measurements.

sequence ↓ energy parameters (Nearest-neighbor, stacking, loop penalties) ↓ candidate structures ↓ optimization ↓ predicted structure

Step 3 — Transcription Kinetics

Modeling the biological process.

RNA polymerase is not simply copying a DNA sequence into an identical string. It is producing a chemically different polymer. A mathematical model of transcription must represent initiation, nucleotide incorporation (using U instead of T), pausing, and termination.

Step 4 — The 3 Levels of Reality

Your goal as a bioinformatician is to move up and down this stack.

Level Description Examples
1. Physical Biology The real molecular system. DNA/RNA chemistry, folding, binding, transcription
2. Representation How we encode biology for computers. A, T, G, C, coordinates, FASTA, FASTQ, GTF
3. Computational Model How we mathematically reason about the encoding. Alignment score, probability, \(\Delta G\), kinetic model

Reference: What Molecular Knowledge Matters?

You don't need to know every chemical detail. You need to know which molecular properties are relevant to the computational problem.

Bioinformatics Task Relevant Molecular Knowledge
FASTQ parsing Sequence representation, orientation, alphabet
Genomic coordinate extraction Genome representation, strand, coordinate conventions
RNA-seq alignment Sequence complementarity, splicing, strandness
RNA structure prediction Base pairing, stacking, thermodynamics
Variant interpretation Sequence context, molecular consequence

The Bioinformatics Bridge

How does representation dictate the outcome of computational tools?

The 7-Question Analytical Framework: RNA-Seq Example

To avoid statistical failure, we answer these 7 questions to map an algorithm (like DESeq2) back to biological reality:

  • What is the biological object? The physical RNA transcript molecules in the cell.
  • What physical process produced the data? Transcription, RNA extraction, and Illumina sequencing.
  • How is that object represented computationally? As digital FASTQ reads, which are then summarized into a Count Matrix.
  • What mathematical model is being used? A Negative Binomial distribution modeling the variance of the counts.
  • What assumptions does the model make? It assumes the genomic coordinates used to extract sequences correctly represent the transcript boundaries.
  • What physical information is lost? 3D folding, transient kinetics, and cellular microenvironment data.
  • Where does the error propagate? If 0-based coordinates are parsed as 1-based, the extracted sequence is wrong, alignment skews, and DESeq2 produces a false-positive p-value.

Differential Expression & Silent Errors

DESeq2 quantifies differential expression based on count matrices. This works because the algorithm relies on the assumption that the upstream transcript quantification accurately represents the biological RNA pool. Without correct upstream representations (e.g., proper strandness or genomic coordinates), DESeq2 is blind to the error. Understanding this ensures you don't mistake a parsing error for a biologically significant upregulated gene with a perfect \(p < 0.05\) volcano plot.

Genomic Coordinate Parsing

BedTools and custom parsers extract sequences using genomic annotations (like `chr7:55086724-55275031`). This works because the genome is linearly indexable. However, if you mistakenly apply a 0-based coordinate system to a 1-based VCF file, you extract the wrong base. Downstream programs will run perfectly on the wrong sequence, creating a mathematically pristine but biologically false result.

RNA Structure Prediction

ViennaRNA predicts the Minimum Free Energy (MFE) secondary structure of an RNA sequence. This works because it calculates \(\Delta G\) using experimentally derived nearest-neighbor thermodynamic parameters. If your input sequence was inadvertently the reverse-complement DNA instead of the actual RNA, the algorithm will still calculate a beautiful hairpin structure—but it will be predicting the structure of the wrong molecule entirely.

The Hidden Cost of Silent Errors

If we fail to properly represent molecular biology computationally:

  • Computational Cost: We waste hours running alignments and DESeq2 on incorrect inputs.
  • Storage Cost: Massive BAM and intermediate files are generated uselessly.
  • Interpretation Cost: We spend weeks investigating a "significant" biological signal that is actually an artifact.
  • Research Cost: Wet-lab experiments are designed around false computational predictions.

Wrong + Program Crashed = Easy to Fix

Wrong + Plausible-Looking = Dangerous Science

\[ \text{Algorithm}(\widehat{\text{Representation}}) \neq \text{Algorithm}(\text{True Biology}) \]

A sophisticated algorithm cannot compensate for an incorrect biological input representation.
