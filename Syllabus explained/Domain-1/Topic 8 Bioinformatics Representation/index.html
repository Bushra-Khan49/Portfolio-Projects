Bioinformatics Representation & Inference

Bioinformatics algorithms do not operate on abstract letters. They operate on models of biological molecules. If your model is biologically wrong, the computational result can be internally consistent but completely false.

Scroll to Explore

Why This Matters

The central dogma (DNA → RNA → Protein) is often taught as a deterministic machine. However, biological systems are inherently probabilistic.

A cell contains \(10^5\) copies of various RNA molecules. When we sequence them, we don't sequence every molecule; we sample a subset. Because of this, observed counts are highly stochastic.

Computational biology rarely deals with absolute certainty. Instead of assuming the observation is the exact biological state, we must model the probability of an observation given a biological state:

\(P(\text{observation} \mid \text{biological state})\)

This transition from deterministic biology to probabilistic statistics is the foundational shift necessary for quantitative bioinformatics.

Why Should a Bioinformatician Care?

A critical conceptual mistake is conflating Error with Uncertainty. They are mathematically and biologically distinct.

Error

You extracted the wrong sequence. If the true sequence is AUGCGUAC but your pipeline outputs AUGCGAC, that is a discrete error.

Uncertainty

The sequencer signal is ambiguous, and the algorithm isn't sure whether a base is A or G. This represents statistical uncertainty.

Robust Bioinformatics Pipeline

Step 1 — The Chain of Representations

Bioinformatics is a chain of representations and inferences. Every transformation introduces the potential for error or uncertainty.

As we move down this chain, information is inherently lost. Converting physical RNA into a FASTA string retains sequence identity but completely discards instantaneous physical state, molecular folding, and transient kinetics.

The Inference Pipeline:

  • 1. Physical Reality: DNA / RNA molecules
  • 2. Experimental Signal: Sequencing optics / voltage
  • 3. Digital Representation: FASTQ / FASTA strings
  • 4. Mathematical Objects: Probabilities, Scores, Counts
  • 5. Computational Conclusion: Expression levels, Variants
  • 6. Biological Interpretation

Step 2 — Assumptions & Plausible Failures

Every method operates on hidden assumptions.

The Silent Error Problem

RNA structure predictors assume specific thermodynamic models and ionic equilibrium. RNA-seq methods assume specific annotations and mapping distributions. If your biological assumption is wrong—for example, treating a kinetically trapped molecule as if it were in thermodynamic equilibrium—then even with an excellent algorithm and perfect code, the result will be completely false.

Excellent Computation + Wrong Model = Precisely Wrong Answer

If the representation is wrong at the top of the chain (e.g. wrong genomic coordinate), it propagates silently through alignment, quantification, and statistics, culminating in a biologically impossible conclusion backed by pristine statistical confidence.

Bioinformatics Connection

To avoid thermodynamic bankruptcy or statistical failure, we must use a rigorous analytical framework. The 7-Question Mental Framework helps map any algorithm back to biological reality.

  1. What is the biological object? (DNA, RNA, Protein, Cell, Population)
  2. What physical process produced the data? (Transcription, Sequencing, Binding, Expression)
  3. How is that object represented computationally? (FASTA, FASTQ, BAM, Count Matrix)
  4. What mathematical model is being used? (Probability, Likelihood, Alignment score, \(\Delta G\))
  5. What assumptions does the model make? (The most neglected question)
  6. What physical information is lost during representation?
  7. If the representation is wrong, where does the error propagate?

Master Systems Overview

"You aren't learning molecular structure as isolated chemistry. You're learning what the computational objects in bioinformatics actually represent, what they leave out, and why errors in those representations can propagate into scientific conclusions."
