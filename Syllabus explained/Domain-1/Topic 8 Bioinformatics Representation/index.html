TOPIC 8

Bioinformatics Representation & Inference

Bioinformatics algorithms do not operate on abstract letters alone. They are models of biological molecules. If your model of the molecule is wrong, the computational result can be internally consistent but biologically wrong.

Scroll to Explore

Why This Matters

A computational error does not necessarily cause a program to crash. A script can be syntactically correct and computationally successful while producing biologically incorrect results.

Understanding representation is crucial to avoiding plausible but wrong conclusions.

What Problem Does This Solve?

It solves the "silent error" problem in bioinformatics pipelines.

By understanding the chain of representations from physical molecule to statistical result, we can accurately diagnose where errors propagate and validate the biological meaning of a computational output.

The Core Mechanics: A Chain of Representation

Bioinformatics is fundamentally a chain of representations and inferences. Every transformation introduces the potential for error or uncertainty.

1. Physical Reality

Object: A physical RNA molecule.

Characteristics: Has a 3D structure, dynamic kinetics, and exists in a specific cellular microenvironment (e.g., pH, salt concentration).

2. Experimental Signal

Object: Fluorophore flashes or voltage changes in a nanopore.

Characteristics: The sequencer observes a physical property of the molecule or its copied fragments. This is a noisy, probabilistic observation.

3. Digital Representation

Object: A string of characters (A, T, G, C, N) in a FASTQ file.

Information Loss: The physical state (structure, kinetics) is completely discarded. All that remains is sequence identity and a quality score indicating the machine's confidence.

4. Mathematical Object

Object: An alignment score, a count matrix, or a probability distribution.

Assumption: The algorithm assumes the digital representation accurately reflects the original molecule. It operates purely on mathematical rules.

5. Computational Conclusion

Object: A p-value indicating differential expression or a called variant.

Vulnerability: If the assumption in step 4 is wrong, the mathematics will still produce a conclusion, but it will be biologically meaningless.

6. Biological Interpretation

The final step where a human (or downstream algorithm) attributes biological meaning to the computational output.

Bioinformatics Connection

To avoid statistical failure, we must map every algorithm back to biological reality using a rigorous analytical framework. You must constantly evaluate the following parameters:

  • Define the biological object (DNA, RNA, Protein, Cell, Population) being analyzed.
  • Identify the physical process that produced the data (Transcription, Sequencing, Binding, Expression).
  • Understand how that object is represented computationally (FASTA, FASTQ, BAM, Count Matrix).
  • Analyze the mathematical model being used (Probability, Likelihood, Alignment score).
  • Audit the assumptions the model makes (This is the most critical and often neglected step).
  • Acknowledge what physical information was lost during representation.
  • Trace where the error propagates if the initial representation is flawed.
