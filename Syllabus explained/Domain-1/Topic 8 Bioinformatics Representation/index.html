TOPIC 8

RNA

Bioinformatics algorithms operate on models of biological molecules. Flawed models yield biologically meaningless results.

Scroll to Explore

Why This Matters

Think back to what we've established about DNA replication and molecular fidelity. The molecules we track—DNA, RNA, and proteins—have complex physical properties, kinetic histories, and thermodynamic constraints.

When you look at a FASTQ file or a sequencing read, it is incredibly tempting to think, "These are just strings of A, T, G, and C." But biologically, they are not just strings. They represent dynamic physical objects.

That distinction becomes dangerously important in computational biology. A computational error does not necessarily cause a program to crash. A script can be syntactically correct, execute flawlessly, and produce a nice volcano plot while harboring a profound biological error.

The Central Hierarchy

Keep this hierarchy in mind throughout bioinformatics:

\[ \text{Molecular system} \rightarrow \text{Physical behavior} \rightarrow \text{Mathematical model} \rightarrow \text{Computational prediction} \]
MOLECULE → BIOLOGICAL PROCESS → EXPERIMENT → SEQUENCE → FILE FORMAT → COMPUTATIONAL OBJECT → ALGORITHM → STATISTICAL RESULT → BIOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION

The Central Hierarchy in Practice

Keep this sequence of translation in mind. For example, in structure prediction:

RNA sequence → RNA structure → \(\Delta G\) → predicted structure

Or in modeling transcription rates:

DNA + Polymerase + NTPs → transcription → kinetic model → predicted transcription rate

What is the Difference Between DNA and RNA?

At the sequence level, you might see ATGCGTAC vs AUGCGUAC. It is tempting to think T ↔ U and therefore DNA and RNA are essentially the same thing. That is only true at a very limited information-representation level.

The most important structural difference is the sugar backbone. DNA contains deoxyribose (2'-H). RNA contains ribose (2'-OH).

That single hydroxyl group has enormous physical consequences. It alters hydrogen bonding, hydration shells, local geometry, and chemical reactivity. It is the reason RNA tends to have different conformational possibilities from DNA.

RNA Structural Complexity

DNA most commonly exists as a relatively stable double-stranded helix. RNA, however, is frequently single-stranded, but that single strand can fold back onto itself.

5' ────GGG─────CCC──── 3' \ / \___/

The sequence itself contains information that allows different parts of the molecule to interact. So the computational problem becomes:

sequence → possible intramolecular interactions → structure

Base Pairing

When people first learn nucleic-acid stability, they often focus on A-U and G-C pairs, thinking: "G-C has three hydrogen bonds, so it must always be more stable." That is incomplete. RNA stability also depends heavily on base stacking.

Adjacent aromatic bases can interact through stacking interactions.

Base Stacking

Therefore a simplified picture of RNA stability is:

\[ \text{stability} \sim \text{base pairing} + \text{stacking} + \text{loop effects} + \text{solvent effects} + \text{ions} + \text{conformational effects} \]

This is why serious RNA structure prediction doesn't simply count G-C and A-U pairs.

Nearest-Neighbor Effects & Dynamic Programming

The energetic contribution of one base pair can depend on the bases immediately adjacent to it. Therefore RNA folding algorithms often use nearest-neighbor thermodynamic parameters.

The algorithm evaluates \(\Delta G(S)\) for thousands of structures to find the minimum free energy structure: \( S^* = \arg\min_S \Delta G(S) \).

Because the number of possible structures grows exponentially, the algorithm uses Dynamic Programming—solving smaller subproblems (like small loops) and reusing their results. So an RNA structure predictor combines RNA thermodynamics + combinatorial optimization + dynamic programming.

The 3 Conceptual Families of Equations

Rather than memorizing every equation independently, organize them into three conceptual families: Thermodynamics, Kinetics, and Computational Modeling.

Thermodynamics & Gibbs Free Energy (ΔG)

Which molecular state is thermodynamically favored? The probability of a state existing is determined by the balance of energy and entropy.

\[ \Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S \]

Let's break down these symbols:

  • \(\Delta G\) (Gibbs Free Energy): The total energy "cost" of changing states. If favorable, \(\Delta G < 0\).
  • \(\Delta H\) (Enthalpy): Captures changes in energetic interactions (hydrogen bonds, stacking, solvent interactions). If favorable, \(\Delta H < 0\).
  • \(\Delta S\) (Entropy): Reflects how many microscopic states are accessible. A folded RNA is constrained, so folding carries an entropic penalty (\(\Delta S < 0\)).
  • \(T\) (Temperature): Represents the thermal heat constantly bumping into the molecules. Higher temperature makes folding less favorable.

What is the Boltzmann Distribution?

How does a slight energy difference influence the population of molecular states? The answer lies in the Boltzmann Distribution.

\[ P_i = \frac{e^{-G_i/(RT)}}{\sum_j e^{-G_j/(RT)}} \]

Let's break down these symbols:

  • \(P_i\) (Probability): The likelihood that a specific conformation will form and stay together.
  • \(e\) (Euler's number): A mathematical constant used for exponential growth.
  • \(G_i\) (Gibbs Free Energy): The total energy cost of that specific state.
  • \(R\) (Gas constant) & \(T\) (Temperature): Represents the thermal energy scale.

Think of it like a valley. An algorithm might find the single structure with the lowest \(\Delta G\) (the deepest valley). But real RNA molecules populate an ensemble of conformations. A state in a deep, stable valley has a high probability, but states in shallower valleys still have nonzero probability. The enzyme doesn't actively "decide"—it simply lets thermal physics do the filtering.

Kinetics: Nucleotide Incorporation

Thermodynamics asks: Which state is energetically favored? Kinetics asks: How quickly does the system move between states?

What chemical event corresponds to nucleotide incorporation?

\[ NTP + RNA_n \rightarrow RNA_{n+1} + PP_i \]

Let's break down these symbols:

  • \(NTP\): A free nucleotide tri-phosphate.
  • \(RNA_n\): The growing RNA chain of length n.
  • \(PP_i\): Pyrophosphate (the terminal two phosphates that leave, breaking bonds to drive transcription).

Kinetics: Polymerase Velocity

How quickly is RNA being produced?

\[ v = \frac{d[RNA]}{dt} \]

Let's break down these symbols:

  • \(v\): The velocity or rate of the reaction.
  • \(\frac{d}{dt}\): The mathematical derivative, representing how rapidly concentration changes with time.
  • \([RNA]\): The concentration of RNA.

Think of it like a car's speedometer. Polymerase speed depends on nucleotide availability, template sequence, pausing, and termination. The derivative simply reads the speedometer at a given moment in time.

Computational Modeling

How similar are two sequences according to our chosen scoring model?

\[ Score = \sum s(x_i,y_i) + \text{gap penalties} \]

Let's break down these symbols:

  • \(\sum\) (Sigma): Summing up all the individual scores across the sequence.
  • \(s(x_i,y_i)\): The substitution score for aligning character \(x_i\) with character \(y_i\).
  • \(\text{gap penalties}\): Deductions for introducing insertions or deletions.

Think of it like grading a test with a rubric. Real alignment algorithms distinguish between gap opening and gap extension. The algorithm isn't measuring chemical energy; it is estimating sequence similarity according to a mathematical model, which serves as a proxy for biological homology.

The Most Abstract Equations: Representation & Error

You have the actual biological object (\(X\)). But we cannot put a physical molecule into a computer. We create a representation (\(\hat{X}\)).

\[ \hat{X} \approx X \]

The FASTA sequence is \(\hat{X}\). It lacks atoms, bonds, solvent, and dynamics. We hope \(\hat{X} \approx X\) for the question we are asking. "Good representation" means representation appropriate to the question.

What happens when the input is wrong?

Your computational method \(f\) transforms an input into an output (\(Y = f(X)\)). But your pipeline uses the representation \(\hat{X}\). So your output becomes:

\[ \hat{Y} = f(\hat{X}) \]

The difference between what you wanted and what you got is the error:

\[ \hat{Y} - Y = f(\hat{X}) - f(X) \]

If a coordinate parsing error deletes one nucleotide from \(\hat{X}\), your structure predictor \(f\) will execute perfectly and calculate a \(\Delta G\) for \(\hat{X}\). The algorithm worked flawlessly, but the output belongs to a molecule that doesn't physically exist.

Connection to Bioinformatics

You don't need to know every chemical detail of DNA and RNA before doing bioinformatics. Rather, you need to know which molecular properties are relevant to the computational problem you are solving.

Bioinformatics Task Molecular Knowledge That Matters
FASTQ Parsing Sequence representation, orientation, alphabet
Genomic Coordinate Extraction Genome representation, strand, coordinate conventions
RNA-Seq Alignment Sequence complementarity, splicing, strandness
Transcript Quantification Transcript structure, isoforms, annotation
RNA Structure Prediction Base pairing, stacking, thermodynamics
Protein Structure Prediction Amino-acid chemistry, folding, energetics

Pipeline Realities: FASTA vs FASTQ & Coordinates

FASTA vs FASTQ (Error vs Uncertainty)

FASTA represents sequence identity. FASTQ represents sequence + measurement uncertainty. The FASTQ quality score (\(Q\)) is translated mathematically: \(P_e = 10^{-Q/10}\). A read isn't simply a string; it is an observation with quantitative uncertainty. Bioinformatics must minimize error (extracting the wrong sequence) while propagating uncertainty (how much we trust a base call).

Sequence Orientation & Strandness

A sequence 5' - ATGC - 3' has a reverse complement 5' - GCAT - 3'. If your pipeline ignores strand information in RNA-seq, it can assign reads to the wrong overlapping gene on the opposite strand. Orientation is not cosmetic; it is physical reality.

Coordinate Systems & Splicing

There are two major coordinate conventions: 1-based inclusive and 0-based half-open. That one-character difference creates an off-by-one error, potentially shifting the reading frame of every downstream codon. Furthermore, genomic coordinate space \(\neq\) transcript coordinate space due to splicing. RNA-seq alignment isn't finding a string; it is finding where RNA originated across discontinuous genomic regions.

Sequence Representation in RNA-Seq

DESeq2 identifies differentially expressed genes by analyzing count matrices. This works because the algorithm assumes the count matrix represents accurate genomic annotations extracted upstream. If your pipeline accidentally extracted sequences from the wrong strand or used a 0-based coordinate system incorrectly, the sequence representation is flawed. DESeq2 does not know this; it will happily process the flawed counts and output a statistically significant, biologically false result.

Thermodynamics in Structure Prediction

Algorithms like ViennaRNA predict secondary structures by minimizing free energy. This works because it calculates \(\Delta G\) using rigorous thermodynamic parameters derived for RNA chemistry. If your input is inadvertently DNA—or if you fail to account for ionic conditions—the algorithm will still execute perfectly and draw a beautiful hairpin structure. However, because the underlying molecular representation is wrong, the structure belongs to a molecule that doesn't physically exist.

Coordinate Parsing & Sequence Extraction

Tools like BedTools rely entirely on strict coordinate conventions. A genome annotation (e.g., chr7:55,086,724-55,275,031) seems purely computational. But those coordinates map to physical DNA. If you parse an inclusive boundary as exclusive, you shift the reading frame. The downstream FASTA file is generated flawlessly, yet the biological object it represents has been completely corrupted.

The Mental Framework: Mapping to Physical Reality

Whenever you encounter a new bioinformatics method, you must explicitly map it back to physical reality by establishing seven crucial parameters:

  • Biological Object Identification: Clearly define whether the algorithm models DNA, RNA, Protein, a Cell, or a Population.
  • Data Generation Physics: Establish the physical process that produced the data, such as Transcription, Sequencing, or Binding interactions.
  • Computational Abstraction: Identify how the object is represented digitally, whether through FASTA, FASTQ, BAM, or a Count Matrix.
  • Mathematical Translation: Determine the specific mathematical model governing the computation, such as \(\Delta G\), Probability logic, or an Alignment Score.
  • Model Assumptions: Explicitly declare the hidden assumptions the model makes about the biology, which is often the most critical point of failure.
  • Information Attrition: Acknowledge the physical information lost during representation, such as losing physical molecular dynamics when converting to a flat FASTA string.
  • Error Propagation: Trace precisely where errors will propagate in the pipeline if the initial molecular representation is chemically or physically incorrect.

The Deepest Bioinformatics Lesson

REAL BIOLOGY ↓ incorrect representation X̂ ↓ correct algorithm ↓ correct computation ↓ incorrect biological conclusion

Excellent computation + Wrong model = Precisely wrong answer

If Flawless Understanding Did Not Exist

The cost of a silent error is profound. If we fail to properly represent the molecular biology:

  • Computational Cost: We waste days running alignments and enrichment analyses on corrupted inputs.
  • Storage Cost: Massive BAM files are generated and stored unnecessarily.
  • Interpretation Cost: We spend precious time investigating a biological signal that isn't real.
  • Scientific Cost: A wrong computational assumption becomes a published, incorrect biological claim.

Wrong + Program Crashed = Easy to fix.

Wrong + Plausible-Looking = Dangerous science.

"A sophisticated algorithm cannot compensate for an incorrect biological input representation unless it explicitly models that uncertainty."
